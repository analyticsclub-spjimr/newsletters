01MARKETS

Nvidia brings back Jensen Huang, 11 days after he quit

Nvidia’s board unanimously brought back Jensen Huang as chief executive on 23 November, just 11 days after he quit. He had resigned amid reports about his personal relationships and about whether foreign intelligence had targeted him. Six days before the reversal, the board’s chair said Huang would not return. After input from senior US officials, the board concluded his return posed no national-security risk. President Trump, who had urged the move, called it “correct and proper”.

Why it matters

Investors call this key-person risk, and the market just put a price on it. Nvidia’s shares jumped 31.5% that day, their biggest one-day gain on record. Since their 6 October peak they had lost nearly half their value, hit by an AI sell-off, Huang’s exit and a Reuters report that SEC staff were examining possible accounting problems. The board’s inquiry is still open.

02SCIENCE

AI may have helped crack maths’ most famous unsolved problem

On 20 November, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge said he had used Claude to help prove the Riemann hypothesis, a 167-year-old problem that carries a $1 million prize. Instead of a traditional paper, he released the proof as roughly 17 million lines of Lean, a programming language in which a computer checks every logical step. Early checks have passed. The same day, a Stanford researcher posted a proof done without AI, which is also still being checked.

Why it matters

If the proof holds up, AI will have helped solve a problem that stumped mathematicians for 167 years, with an answer no human yet fully understands. Banks already deal with a smaller version of this: risk models whose outputs can be tested even when their reasoning is hard to explain.

Mathematicians have not yet accepted the claim. Martin Bridson, head of the Clay Mathematics Institute, which awards the prize, says real understanding “will be long and difficult”.

03TOOLS

OpenAI cuts prices with GPT-6.2 as Google launches Gemini 4 Neon

OpenAI released GPT-6.2 on 17 November in two tiers: Astra, built for heavy lifting, and Sol, a cheaper option for everyday tasks. For developers, Astra is 20% cheaper than GPT-6 Astra. A day later, Google unveiled Gemini 4 Neon, which slots between its most powerful model and its fastest one. Google says Neon tops Artificial Analysis’s index of AI coding agents.

Why it matters

Top models keep getting cheaper for the same work, so a company choosing AI tools should compare what a finished task costs as well as where a model ranks.

04FINANCE

Anthropic completes the largest stock-market listing ever

Anthropic, the maker of Claude, priced its shares at $83 on 18 November, above the range it had set. Including extra shares bought by its underwriters, the listing raised $93.5 billion, beating SpaceX’s June listing as the largest ever, and valued Anthropic at about $1.5 trillion. Its shares began trading on Nasdaq the next day.

Why it matters

It is the first of the big US AI labs to go public, so investors can now price one every trading day. The swings started on day one: the shares opened 34% above the offer price and closed 17% up.

05REGULATION

Europe’s AI-label deadline is 2 December; India’s rule is already live

By 2 December, AI tools sold in Europe must tag what they make, text included, so that software can tell it was made by AI. Tools launched after 2 August 2026 have had to do this from the start. The same day, the EU will ban AI tools that make non-consensual fake intimate images of real people. India has had its own rule since 20 February: platforms must label realistic AI-made audio, images and video, and add permanent metadata where feasible.

Why it matters

Indian firms selling AI tools in Europe now face two overlapping sets of rules. Europe’s target AI makers and include text; India’s target platforms and cover audio, images and video. A single compliance plan can handle both.

India watch: rules for consent managers under the data protection law took effect on 13 November, with most other obligations starting on 13 May 2027. The AI rules paper that IT Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw promised on 8 October “within a month” is still not out; on 25 November, officials said it was in its final stages.

06LAW

A Supreme Court judge wants 24/7 AI divorce courts

Speaking in Jabalpur on 21 November, Supreme Court judge Joymalya Bagchi proposed shifting mutual-consent divorces to “24/7 AI divorce courts”. With more than five crore cases pending in India, he argued, judges should not spend time on such “non-adjudicatory” work. He also suggested using AI to predict likely verdicts in cheque-bounce and money-recovery disputes before arguments begin, while insisting that constitutional cases stay in human hands.

Why it matters

In September, the Supreme Court threw out a ₹425 crore customs penalty because the order cited non-existent judgments, apparently invented by AI, and warned that AI can serve as “training wheels” but not as the pilot.

07HEALTHCARE

Big drugmakers say AI has cut drug discovery time by up to half

Scientists at AstraZeneca, Novartis, Sanofi, Novo Nordisk and other big drugmakers told Reuters on 20 November that AI has cut the time needed to design new molecules and move them into clinical trials by up to half this year. Novartis said its average time from project start to drug candidate has shrunk from about three years to 18 to 24 months, and Sanofi said it saved at least a year on an asthma drug.

Why it matters

Faster, cheaper early research could change how drugmakers and investors value drug pipelines. But these figures are the companies’ own, and historically nearly nine in ten drugs that enter clinical trials fail, so quicker discovery does not automatically mean more approved medicines.

08SECURITY

Attackers target Indian payment systems and lenders

On 20 November, India’s cybersecurity agency CERT-In warned that attackers were altering payment records and approval chains at firms that handle government-linked payments and vendor payouts, and flagged AI tools that scan for weak spots. Separately, Delhi Police said hackers had diverted more than ₹65 crore in approved loans from at least 12 non-banking lenders, and investigators found traces of AI use. It follows the AI agents we reported in Issue 1, which read data inside Indian organisations without anyone noticing.

Why it matters

Fraud is shifting from tricking customers to tampering with the systems that approve payments. That makes approval steps and vendor access a business problem as much as an IT one.

09START-UPS

Sarvam sells India-hosted AI, priced in rupees

On 16 November, Bengaluru-based Sarvam AI launched Token Factory, a service that runs AI models on processors in India and bills in rupees. Alongside Sarvam’s own 105-billion-parameter model, it hosts open models such as DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM and Gemma; DeepSeek V4.1 Flash launched at ₹14 per million input tokens. When critics asked why a start-up backed by the IndiaAI Mission was serving mostly Chinese models, co-founder Pratyush Kumar said the service runs on processors Sarvam bought itself, not on subsidised government computing.

Why it matters

Local hosting and rupee billing solve two big headaches for Indian businesses. Because most of the models on offer are Chinese, though, buyers in sensitive sectors will still have to decide whether they are comfortable using them.

10CAREERS

AI isn’t wiping out India’s IT jobs, but freshers are feeling it

Fears that AI will hollow out India’s outsourcing industry the way Chinese factories hit Western manufacturing are “overdone”, says a Deutsche Bank report released on 19 November. Staffing firm Xpheno counted 130,000 tech job openings in November, up 27% on a year earlier. Yet fresher hiring is slowing, and TCS has 23,460 fewer employees than a year ago. In the US, Goldman Sachs says the feared wave of AI layoffs did not arrive this year.

Why it matters

For MBA graduates, the bigger risk is a tighter pool of entry-level roles. Deutsche Bank also warns that companies pausing junior hiring today could face a shortage of senior talent later.

ThePrint (Deutsche Bank report) ↗Fortune (Goldman Sachs) ↗
11MARKETING

AI-made ads look cheap to many Indians, but they still win clicks

In an Accenture survey released on 18 November, 56% of Indian respondents said brands that use AI for content or ads look “lazy, careless and cheap”, but the click data says otherwise. A Taboola study with Columbia and Harvard researchers, covering 500 million ad views, found that AI ads did as well as human-made ones, and the AI ads that looked most natural drew the most clicks. In a live Instagram test, AI images even drew about 50% more clicks than images by a professional designer.

Why it matters

Polished AI ads hold their own, while obviously artificial ones lose clicks. From 29 December, the Advertising Standards Council of India also requires labels on AI-made ads that could mislead viewers.
