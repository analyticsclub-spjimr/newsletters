01MARKETS

Nvidia brings back Jensen Huang, 11 days after he quit

Nvidia’s board unanimously reinstated Jensen Huang as chief executive on 23 November, 11 days after he resigned following reports about his personal relationships and whether he had been targeted by foreign intelligence. Six days earlier, its chair had said Huang would not return. After input from senior US officials, the board said his return posed no national-security risk. President Trump, who had urged the move, called it “correct and proper”.

Why it matters

Investors call this key-person risk, and Nvidia just priced it. The shares jumped by about a third, after losing nearly half their value since 6 October to an AI sell-off, Huang’s exit and a Reuters report that SEC staff were examining possible accounting problems. The board’s inquiry is still open.

02SCIENCE

AI may have helped crack maths’ most famous unsolved problem

On 20 November, Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said he had found a proof of the Riemann hypothesis with Claude’s help. The 167-year-old problem carries a $1 million prize. Instead of a paper, he released some 17 million lines of Lean, code that a computer can check step by step, and early checks have passed. The same day, a Stanford researcher posted an all-human proof, still being checked.

Why it matters

If it holds, AI will have helped crack a problem that beat mathematicians for 167 years, with a proof no human yet fully understands. Banks already live with a smaller version of this: risk models whose answers can be tested but are hard to explain.

Disclosure: Alpöge works at Anthropic, which makes Claude, and this issue was drafted with help from Claude. Mathematicians have not yet accepted the claim. Martin Bridson, who heads the Clay Institute that awards the prize, says real understanding “will be long and difficult”.

03TOOLS

OpenAI cuts prices with GPT-6.2 as Google launches Gemini 4 Neon

OpenAI released GPT-6.2 on 17 November in two versions: Astra, for the hardest work, and Sol, a cheaper model for everyday tasks. For developers, Astra costs 20% less than GPT-6 Astra. A day later, Google released Gemini 4 Neon, which sits between its most powerful and fastest models; Google says it ranks first on Artificial Analysis’s index of AI coding agents.

Why it matters

Top models keep getting cheaper for the same work, so a company choosing AI tools should compare what a finished task costs as well as where a model ranks.

04FINANCE

Anthropic completes the largest stock-market listing ever

Anthropic, maker of the Claude chatbot, priced its initial public offering at $83 a share on 18 November, above its range of $70 to $78. Selling 980 million shares raised about $81.3 billion, beating SpaceX’s June listing as the largest ever, and the total reached $93.5 billion after underwriters bought 147 million extra shares. That valued the company at about $1.5 trillion. Its shares began trading on Nasdaq the next day.

Why it matters

It is the first of the big US AI labs to list, so investors can now price one every trading day. The price swung hard on day one: the shares opened 34% above the offer price and closed 17% up.

05REGULATION

Europe’s AI-label deadline is 2 December; India’s rule is already live

Under the EU’s AI Act, generative AI tools sold before 2 August 2026 must mark their output, text included, in a machine-readable way by 2 December; newer tools have had to since launch. The same day, an EU ban on AI tools that make fake intimate images of real people without consent takes effect. India’s rule, in force since 20 February, makes platforms label realistic AI-made audio, images and video and, where feasible, add permanent metadata.

Why it matters

Indian firms selling AI tools in Europe face two sets of rules. The EU’s apply to AI makers and include text; India’s apply to platforms and include audio and video. A single compliance plan can handle both.

India watch: rules for consent managers under the data protection law took effect on 13 November; most other duties start on 13 May 2027. The AI rules paper that IT Minister Ashwini Vaishnaw promised on 8 October “within a month” is still not out.

06LAW

A Supreme Court judge wants 24/7 AI divorce courts

Speaking in Jabalpur on 21 November, Supreme Court judge Joymalya Bagchi proposed moving mutual-consent divorces to “24/7 AI divorce courts”, so judges’ time is not spent on “non-adjudicatory” work while India sits on over five crore pending cases. He also suggested AI predict likely verdicts in cheque-bounce and money-recovery cases before arguments begin, but said constitutional cases must stay with humans.

Why it matters

In September, the Supreme Court scrapped a ₹425 crore customs penalty because the order cited judgments that don’t exist, apparently made up by AI. AI can be “training wheels”, the court said, but not the pilot.

07HEALTHCARE

Big drugmakers say AI has cut drug discovery time by up to half

Scientists at AstraZeneca, Novartis, Sanofi, Novo Nordisk and others told Reuters on 20 November that AI has cut the time it takes to design new molecules and move them into clinical trials by up to half this year. Novartis said its average time from project start to drug candidate is now 18 to 24 months, down from about three years. Sanofi said it saved at least a year on an asthma drug.

Why it matters

If this holds, early research gets faster and cheaper, which would change how drugmakers and their investors value it. But the figures are the companies’ own, and historically nearly nine in ten drugs that enter clinical trials fail, so faster discovery does not yet mean more medicines.

08SECURITY

Attackers target Indian payment systems and lenders

Issue 1 reported AI agents reading data inside Indian organisations unnoticed. On 20 November, CERT-In, India’s cybersecurity agency, warned that attackers were changing payment records and approval chains at firms handling government-linked payments and vendor payouts, and flagged AI tools that scan for weak spots. Delhi Police said hackers had diverted more than ₹65 crore of approved loans from at least 12 non-banking lenders, and found traces of AI use.

Why it matters

Fraud is moving from tricking customers to editing records inside the systems that approve payments. That makes approval steps and vendor access a business problem as much as an IT one.

09START-UPS

Sarvam sells India-hosted AI, priced in rupees

On 16 November, Bengaluru-based Sarvam AI launched Token Factory, which runs Sarvam’s own 105-billion-parameter model and open models such as DeepSeek, Kimi, GLM and Gemma on processors in India, billed in rupees. DeepSeek V4.1 Flash, for example, costs ₹14 per million input tokens at launch. Critics asked why a company backed by the IndiaAI Mission was serving mostly Chinese models. Co-founder Pratyush Kumar said it runs on privately bought processors, not subsidised government computing.

Why it matters

Keeping data in India and billing in rupees eases two worries for Indian businesses. Most of the models on offer are Chinese, though, so buyers in sensitive sectors will have to decide whether to trust them.

10CAREERS

AI isn’t wiping out India’s IT jobs, but freshers are feeling it

A Deutsche Bank report released on 19 November calls fears that AI will do to India’s outsourcing jobs what Chinese factories did to Western manufacturing “overdone”. Tech job openings reached 130,000 in November, up 27% on a year earlier, according to staffing firm Xpheno. But fresher hiring is slowing, and TCS has 23,460 fewer employees than a year ago. In the US, Goldman Sachs says the feared AI layoff wave did not arrive this year.

Why it matters

For MBA graduates, the bigger risk is a thinner pool of entry-level openings. Deutsche Bank also warns that firms which stop hiring juniors now may run short of senior people later.

ThePrint (Deutsche Bank report) ↗Fortune (Goldman Sachs) ↗
11MARKETING

AI-made ads look cheap to many Indians, but they still win clicks

In Accenture’s survey released on 18 November, 56% of Indian respondents said brands using AI for content or ads look “lazy, careless and cheap”, though click data points the other way. A Taboola study with Columbia and Harvard researchers, covering 500 million ad views, found AI ads did as well as human ones, and those that didn’t look AI-made drew the most clicks. In a live Instagram test, AI images drew about 50% more clicks than a professional designer’s.

Why it matters

Polished AI ads hold their own, while ones that look artificial lose clicks. From 29 December, the Advertising Standards Council of India also requires labels on AI-made ads that could mislead.
